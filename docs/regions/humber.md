# The Humber

*The region where the reconstruction is weakest after the Fens, and where the scoping
exercise repeatedly found the network breaking. Humberhead went from 14% unreached to 21%
and back to 3% in a single afternoon's work.*

> **Research leads, not verified citations.** Nothing below has been checked against a
> copy. Dates given as decades are firmer than dates given as years.

## The schemes, and when

| period | scheme | what it did to the network |
|---|---|---|
| 13th c. onwards | [Commissions of Sewers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_of_sewers){:target="_blank"}; piecemeal embanking | maintenance of existing channels; little new cutting |
| **1626–c.1630** | **[Vermuyden's drainage of Hatfield Chase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatfield_Chase){:target="_blank"}** for Charles I, financed by the Participants | the defining intervention: the Don's southern arm to the Trent blocked, all water thrown north to the Aire |
| **1633** | **[The Dutch River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_River){:target="_blank"}**, cut to the Ouse at Goole | a wholly new channel, made because the 1626 works flooded Fishlake and Sykehouse |
| 1635–1637 | Sir John Monson's Ancholme Level; the New River Ancholme | |
| 1628–1650s | [Isle of Axholme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Axholme){:target="_blank"} commoners' resistance; works broken and repaired | channels cut, destroyed and recut — **dates in this window are unstable by nature** |
| 1764 | Holderness Drainage Act | the Hull valley |
| 1772 | Market Weighton Canal | drainage as much as navigation |
| 1798–c.1810 | Beverley and Barmston Drain | |
| c.1802 | Keyingham Level | Holderness |
| late 18th–19th c. | **Warping** — deliberate tidal silt deposition | warping drains, entirely artificial, and numerous |
| 1930 | Land Drainage Act | the modern Internal Drainage Boards |

The concentration is unmistakable: **the network's shape in this region is largely a
product of 1626–1637**, with a second wave of statutory drainage a century and a half
later.

## Contemporary and near-contemporary

- **[Dugdale, William, *The History of Imbanking and Drayning of Divers Fenns and Marshes* (1662)](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_the-history-of-imbanking_dugdale-sir-william_1662){:target="_blank"}** — the foundational contemporary account, covering Hatfield Chase and the Isle of
  Axholme as well as the Fens, and carrying maps. A second edition followed in 1772. **The
  single most important item on the list.**
- **[Abraham de la Pryme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_de_la_Pryme){:target="_blank"}'s diary and his manuscript history of Hatfield** (late 17th c.) —
  an observer living on the Level within living memory of the works.
- **Court of Sewers records**, and the **Duchy of Lancaster** records for Hatfield Chase at
  [The National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/){:target="_blank"}. The Chase was Duchy land, which is why its administration is
  unusually well recorded.
- **Hatfield Chase Corporation** archives (the Participants' successor body), with material
  at Doncaster Archives.

## Modern scholarship and archaeological survey

- **[The Humber Wetlands Project](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/){:target="_blank"}** (Hull / English Heritage, 1990s) — the Humber's
  equivalent of the Fenland Survey, published as regional volumes covering the
  **Humberhead Levels**, **Holderness**, the **Vale of York**, and the **Ancholme and Lower
  Trent valleys**, and synthesised in **Van de Noort, *The Humber Wetlands: The Archaeology
  of a Dynamic Landscape* (2004)**. For palaeochannels and pre-drainage hydrography this is
  the closest thing to a ready-made answer.
- **Gaunt, G.D.** — geological survey work on the Vale of York and Humberhead Levels,
  mapping buried and relict channels.
- **Korthals-Altes, *Sir [Cornelius Vermuyden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Vermuyden){:target="_blank"}* (1925)** and **Harris, *Vermuyden and the
  Fens* (1953)** — biographies covering the engineering.
- **Hunter, Joseph, *South Yorkshire* (1828–31)** and **Tomlinson, *The Level of Hatfield
  Chace* (1882)** — antiquarian topographies with local detail and often with maps.

## Cartographic and land-record

This is where the land-appropriation argument applies most directly, because the Humber
schemes were **contractual**: the Participants took land in exchange for draining it, and
that exchange had to be mapped.

- **Participants' allotment maps and the drainage awards** for Hatfield Chase.
- **Isle of Axholme enclosure awards** (from the 1790s), which post-date the drainage but
  frequently depict older channels as boundaries.
- **Warping drain records**, 18th- and 19th-century — and a good *negative* index: a
  warping drain is certainly not medieval.
- **County maps before the Ordnance Survey** — Warburton (1720) and Jeffreys (1771–72) for
  Yorkshire — for the state of the network between Vermuyden and the OS.
- **[OS six-inch first series](https://maps.nls.uk/os/6inch-england-and-wales/){:target="_blank"}**, which for this region may pre-date some of the
  nineteenth-century warping and later cuts.

## Why this region repays the effort

Two reasons beyond its weakness in the current data. The interventions are **densely dated
and legally documented**, so a course can often be pinned to a decade rather than a
century. And the Don's diversion is a case where the modern channel is not a modification
of the medieval one but a **replacement of it** — the water was sent an entirely different
way in 1626. No amount of patching the modern survey will recover that; only the
documentary and cartographic record will.
