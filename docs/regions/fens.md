# The Fens

[← The rebuilt landscapes](index)

*The landscape this project exists for, and the one where the standard method fails on
first principles.*

> **Research leads, not verified citations**, except where marked **verified**.

## Why the modern channel is worthless here

Everywhere else in England the modern river is a modified version of the older one. In
the Great Level it is not: the seventeenth-century cuts are **new channels on new lines**,
the old watercourses were abandoned rather than improved, and the peat surface they ran
across has since **wasted by several metres** — so even the terrain no longer records
where the water went.

The scoping exercise measured the consequence directly. Before the Fen drains were
excluded, it routed **17.8 m³/s of the Great Ouse at 1348** down the Hundred Foot Drain
(1600), the Old Bedford (1631) and the River Delph (1651), because no pre-1348 channel in
the network reached the sea. The medieval answer — the Wellstream and the Old Croft River
— was drawn on the map and connected to nothing.

That is the failure mode this region produces: not an obviously wrong line, but a
confident number carried down a channel that did not yet exist.

## Contemporary and near-contemporary

- **Jonas Moore, *A Mapp of ye Great Levell of ye Fenns* (1658)** — the most immediately
  actionable cartographic source in the whole project: a survey of the Level *as the works
  were being carried out*. It survives in several forms, including sheets at [The National
  Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/){:target="_blank"} and a single-sheet reproduction, and georeferencing control points for it were
  already curated during the scoping exercise.
- **[Dugdale, *The History of Imbanking and Drayning* (1662)](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_the-history-of-imbanking_dugdale-sir-william_1662){:target="_blank"}** — the contemporary account
  of the Adventurers' works, with maps. Shared with [the Humber](humber).
- **[Wells, *The History of the Drainage of the Great Level of the Fens* (1829)](https://archive.org/details/historydrainage01wellgoog){:target="_blank"}** — a
  documentary history of what was cut and when. Identified and unexploited.

## Modern scholarship and survey

- **[Darby, *The Draining of the Fens* (1940)](https://archive.org/details/drainingoffens0000hcda){:target="_blank"}** and *The Changing Fenland* (1983) —
  **verified**, and the authority for most of the datings above.
- **The Fenland Survey** — Hall and Coles, *Fenland Survey: An Essay in Landscape and
  Persistence* (1994) — **verified** for the lodes, and the model for what a systematic
  wetland survey yields: roddons, buried channels and settlement, mapped.
- **Roddon mapping.** The silt-filled beds of the pre-drainage watercourses stand *proud*
  of the wasted peat and are visible from the air. **They are the single best physical
  evidence of the medieval network anywhere in England, and no attempt has yet used
  them.** High-resolution terrain data covers them; the scoping exercise proved the
  extraction on one channel and went no further.

## Cartographic and land-record

- **Adventurers' allotments and the drainage awards.** As in the Humber, land was taken in
  exchange for the drainage, and the exchange had to be mapped.
- **Enclosure and tithe maps.** Available, but under end-user agreements that bar
  redistribution — usable to inform a judgement, never to travel in a published file.
- **Sea level and the coastline.** Transcribed Holocene relative sea-level curves for the
  Wash exist, and the medieval shore here runs kilometres inland of the modern one. It is
  treated as a **measurement to be drawn, never a filter to clip the network with**.

## What to do here first

**Trace the Wellstream and the Old Croft River**, from Moore 1658 and the roddon evidence.
They are the medieval outfall of the Great Ouse, the reason the seventeenth-century cuts
have to be excluded, and the single largest hole in the reconstruction.
